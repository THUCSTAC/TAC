# 2026 Autumn · New-TA Peer Sharing

The first training of the semester for new TAs, a panel with experienced TAs from various courses (hosted by Chen Jiajie, with several TA guests), online/offline combined, with recordings provided for makeup study. This session focused on **the impact of AI on course teaching and how to respond**, **AI applications in TA work**, **AI-oriented adjustments to course content and assessment**, and the operation experience of the fully AI-taught course **"Toward General AI"**.

## Session flow

- Introduce the panel guests
- New TAs scan the **sign-in questionnaire**
- During the session, scan the **"real-time question questionnaire"**, which syncs to an online spreadsheet; the host periodically switches to the sheet and invites the sharing TAs to answer briefly
- **"Toward General AI" sharing**
- A makeup-study notice for absent TAs will be posted in the TA group

## Discussion topics (mainly guest sharing + real-time questions)

- **What impact has AI had on the teaching of the course you TA for?**
- **If students submit a large amount of AI-generated, low-quality questions or content, how would you handle it?**
- **Have you actively applied AI in your TA work? If so, how exactly?**
- **Has the course you TA for made adjustments for AI? If so, what adjustments, and how well did they work?**
- **What have you learned from students in the course of being a TA?**

## Session notes

### 1. The impact of AI on course teaching and how to respond

**The impact of AI on assessment:**

- **Widespread ghost-writing of programming and math assignments**: students commonly use AI to complete programming and math assignments, making it hard for TAs to tell, so students' real mastery can't be accurately assessed.
- **"Perfect assignments, failing exams" contrast**: AI-assisted work is of very high quality, but the lack of independent thinking leads to a sharp drop in exam performance — a break in the learning curve.

**Concrete strategies against AI ghost-writing:**

- **Reintroduce subjective and "white-box" points**: add subjective grading back, and deduct points in a targeted way from students who merely paste AI chat logs without independent thinking.
- **Increase handwritten work and quizzes**: in math and similar courses, raise the proportion of quizzes or require handwritten steps, to examine students' real thinking process.
- **Require AI-collaboration records**: require students to submit their AI collaboration session records, to track how and how much they used AI.

### 2. AI applications in TA work

- **AI-assisted preliminary grading**: use AI to write scripts for code-framework analysis and initial scoring; TAs only need to fine-tune on top of the AI judgment, greatly reducing workload.
- **AI-assisted long-text plagiarism detection**: use the new generation of large models to handle long texts directly and automatically compare the overlap of reports and code, reducing manual comparison cost.
- **AI-assisted development of teaching simulators**: in the formal languages and automata course, use AI to develop simulators of computation models so students can intuitively feel the course knowledge.
- **AI-assisted construction of online platforms**: use AI to quickly build a Vivado online platform and a course knowledge-base Q&A system, improving the efficiency of platform building.
- **AI-generated standard reference solutions**: use AI to generate standard-format reference solutions; TAs only need small edits before publishing.

### 3. AI-oriented adjustments to course content and assessment

**Course content leaning toward AI:**

- **AI creation added to the computer-culture basics course**: added human–AI collaborative creation content such as AI image/video/music generation and simple web coding.
- **New advanced AI-programming course**: plan to launch a purely AI-programming course, re-examining how to teach software engineering in the AI era.

**Assessment leaning toward presentation and interaction:**

- **Add course presentation segments**: shift part of the assessment from objective assignments to individual or group presentations, using human-to-human interaction to force students to take responsibility for AI work.
- **Restrict AI use in closed-book exams**: in courses such as computer organization, add closed-book or AI-restricted assessment content to evaluate students' real ability without AI.

### 4. Equity of access to AI models and cost control

- **Design assignments doable with free models**: control assignment difficulty so students using free or basic AI models on the market can also finish; for students without accounts, TAs provide guidance on applying for and using them.
- **Provide basic compute and platform support**: provide students with basic resources such as university compute vouchers, Gom coding sets and Meituan LongCat tokens; labs can also deploy a fallback small model for students to call.
- **Cultivate cost awareness**: require students to tally their AI spending in assignments, prompting them to weigh input against output.

### 5. Sharing on the "Toward General AI" course

**Course overview and fully AI-taught mode:**

- **Scale and positioning**: this general-education course has reached 1,700 enrolled students, positioned as "All about AI, nothing about CS" — it does not teach programming or low-level computing, mainly AI philosophy.
- **AI auto-generated online lectures**: using a system jointly developed by the lab and education, TAs only need to upload slides, and the system automatically generates the lecture script and AI teacher voice, greatly reducing preparation burden.

**Assignment design and assessment mechanism:**

- **AI-native assignment design**: assignments require using AI to write a 10,000-word survey (including hidden-reference hit tests), using AI to fabricate data to explore multimodal perception, and using AI to write a coding agent to develop a web mini-game.
- **LM Judge auto-grading and Rubrics**: use an LLM to auto-grade, with public Rubrics; students are allowed to explore the AI grading mechanism through prompt hacking, as a way to assess mastery of AI.

**Large-scale course operation experience:**

- **Rebuilding educational infrastructure**: at a scale of 1,700 students, the offline AMA assessment was cancelled and communication fell back to email; emphasizes the need to rebuild educational infrastructure such as groups and human–computer interaction.
- **Meeting students' emotional and cognitive needs**: commit to replying to emails within 48 hours, keep holding offline lectures, and provide detailed illustrated tutorials for students with weaker foundations (e.g. humanities students), making sure students are "caught".

### 6. What we learned from students

- Handy computer tips seen while students debug (e.g. typing `cmd` directly in the Windows address bar, GitHub's official `gh` tool).
- Beginners' "genuine confusion" often points to deeper issues in the discipline, and helps TAs find unclear spots in course documentation that can be strengthened.
- Assignments that are done seriously and with human effort stand out in a very obvious way (e.g. a student who implemented a cryptography protocol themselves and attached screenshots), which inspires teaching.
- Strong students are often a valuable source of future TAs or even teachers for the teaching team.
